## What the paper actually does (from the full text)
- Model: per gene, segmented ("broken-line") linear regression of normalised expression on time with k = 0..K breakpoints (default K = 3), fitted with Muggeo's iterative method (R package `segmented`). The model is continuous at each breakpoint: breakpoints are changes of slope, not jumps in level (eq. 1). k = 0 is a single straight line.
- Selection: BIC over k; a segment must contain at least mNS points (default 5), else k is reduced by one; "top dynamic genes" are those whose optimal model has adjusted R^2 above a cutoff (default 0.5; 0.8 used in applications). No gene-level P value or FDR.
- Segment direction: up / down / no-change from the slope's t-statistic referred to a t distribution with one degree of freedom, cutoff cpval (default 0.1; 0.2 for high variance). Patterns (e.g. "up-down", "same-up-down") are used to pick genes; trend vectors can be clustered and tested for enrichment.
- Global summary: breakpoint distribution D_t = number of breakpoints at each time point over all top dynamic genes.
- Input and replicates: normalised expression (no count likelihood); replicate time points allowed as repeated values; "Our method does not require replicate time points". The authors call the data "ordered conditions (e.g. time-course or spatial-course)". "Trendy is designed for experiments with T > 10" and "for more densely sampled biological processes".
- Validation: null simulation from 96 shuffled technical-replicate RNA-seq samples (~10,000 genes): on average 30 genes (median 3) passed adjusted R^2 > 0.5 and 0 passed > 0.8; most false positives when mNS = 2 (Fig. 2). Trend simulation (N = 25, 3 replicates per time point): correct k for 97% (low variance) and 90% (high variance) of genes; correct trend 93% and 84%; breakpoint locations very accurate (Table 1). Applications: HeLa cell cycle (48 time points; 118 top genes; breakpoint peaks at mitosis; 34 "up-down-up-down" genes), axolotl development (17 stages; 9,535 genes at adjusted R^2 > 0.8; breakpoint peaks match known waves; 807 "same-up-down" genes enriched for development). In Barry et al. 2017 the authors compared Trendy peak times between mouse and human neural differentiation; the Conclusion says breakpoints "can be compared across experiments if measured on the same time or spatial scale".
- Plots: breakpoint-distribution bar chart along time with known events marked (Figs 3a, 4a); per-gene segmented fits (Figs 3b, 4b, 5); Shiny viewer (Additional file 1).
- Software: R/Bioconductor `Trendy`, GitHub rhondabacher/Trendy, GPL-3.
- Limitations stated: K and especially mNS matter; not suited to short series; EBSeq-HMM better when a single-step change between two consecutive points is expected. Limitations for us: Gaussian on normalised values; units as observations; no specimen model; continuous broken lines cannot represent a level jump, so a sharp edge appears as two breakpoints around a steep segment, and a pure linear gradient contributes no breakpoints at all.

## Fit to our design (revised after reading the full text)
A cheap descriptive layer (breakpoints and segment directions) for fitted PT curves, and a reminder that cross-species comparison of positions (not levels) has been done with it. It is not a calibrated test, and its breakpoint density cannot by itself decide "continuous vs discrete"; the per-gene step-at-boundaries vs smooth model comparison (recommendation 1) and derivative bands (recommendation 5) carry that weight.
