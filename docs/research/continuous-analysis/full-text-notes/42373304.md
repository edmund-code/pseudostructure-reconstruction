## What the paper actually does (from the full text)
- Model (Methods): per pathway, scale genes (StandardScaler), keep the top ~75% of the pathway's genes by an ANOVA F-statistic against the target (SelectKBest with f_regression for pseudotime, f_classif for cell types; allowed sizes from a fixed grid {1..10, 15, 20, ..., 250}), then train a scikit-learn random forest (20 trees, max depth 20): classifier for cell type (multi-class and one-vs-rest; weighted F1 with inverse class-frequency weights), or regressor for pseudotime min-max scaled to 0-1 per trajectory (score = negative RMSE). Score = median of 10-fold cross-validation scores over cells.
- Null / significance (Methods "Significance evaluation"; Fig. S1): 200 random gene sets per size in the grid, drawn from the dataset's genes (after preprocessing = top 5,000 genes by mean expression), run through the same procedure; IQR outlier filter; a gamma distribution fitted to the 200 scores; the pathway's p-value from the gamma CDF of the closest size; BH across pathways. Significant = FDR < 1% AND effect size > 1.2. Random sets are matched on size only, not on expression level.
- This is a gene-sampling (competitive) null: "are this pathway's genes more predictive than random genes of the same size?", not a self-contained test.
- Effect size (Methods): fold change of the geometric mean expression of pathway genes, cell type vs all other cells, or earliest vs latest 20% of cells along the trajectory; sign gives "enriched"/"depleted".
- Replicates: none. Cells are the units; CV folds are random cells within one dataset. No cross-sample or cross-dataset prediction. Feature selection uses all cells before CV (applied identically to random sets).
- Validation: human hematopoiesis (Ranzoni 2021; 2,646 cells, 12 types, 5 Slingshot trajectories), mouse hematopoiesis (Tusi 2018; 4,751 cells, 8 types, 7 trajectories), zebrafish embryogenesis (Farrell 2018; 29,830 cells, 25 trajectories). Marker sets recover the right cell types (Fig. 2A). About 10% of MSigDB pathways significant in human (3,421) and mouse (1,570). Comparison with GSEA (fold-change-ranked fgsea) and GSDensity shows small overlap between tools (Fig. 5A) and more cell-type-specific and more human-mouse-consistent calls for Phoenix (Fig. 5B-C). No ground-truth simulation and no null-data calibration check (only a KS goodness-of-fit of the gamma to background scores, Fig. S1B).
- Plot types: signed -log10(p) heatmaps of pathways x cell types/trajectories (Figs 3A, 4A, S5, S6); per pathway: UMAP coloured by mean pathway expression, boxplots of mean pathway expression per pseudotime bin, and a histogram of random-set scores with the gamma fit and the pathway's score (Figs 3B-E, 4B-E, 6A-D); Venn diagrams (Figs 2B-C, 5A, 6E-F).
- Software: Python 3.11 package, github.com/NachmaniLab/Phoenix (scikit-learn, scipy, scanpy, gseapy); results portal on shinyapps.io.
- Limitations stated (Discussion): depends on the quality of trajectory and cell-type annotations; random forests give no mechanistic model.
- Limitations for OUR design: cell-level CV with no specimen structure; pseudotime target estimated from the same expression (circular unless genes are held out); random sets not matched on expression level; p-values at FDR 1% over tens of thousands of pathways rely on the gamma tail far beyond 200 null draws; pathways > 250 genes excluded.

## Fit to our design (2-4 lines, revised after reading the full text)
The question Phoenix asks (are a pathway's genes more informative about position than random genes?) is a competitive test, which suits PT where most genes are positional. Its implementation does not fit our replication: cell-level CV in one dataset, size-only matched random sets, and a pseudotime target built from the same genes. Borrow the question and the null-histogram figure; implement it with held-out genes, expression-matched random sets and cross-specimen transfer.
