# Discussion: mouse versus human proximal tubule (draft, 2026-11-10)

Fan-lab style, written for a broad readership. Numbers are from `results_fig5_fig6.md` (Fig. 5) and
`question-bank.md`. Citations in [brackets] are placeholders and must be read in full before use (see
`citation_ledger.md`). Sentences marked [to verify] state textbook physiology not yet checked against a source.

---

Mouse studies are the main source of what is known about how the proximal tubule divides its work along its length,
and they underpin much of renal pharmacology. Whether that layout holds in human has been hard to test. Human
kidney atlases resolve the PT into two or three segments [Lake 2023], and comparisons between species have measured
how much of each transporter is present rather than where it sits [human cortex proteomics, PMC5118637; cross-species proteomics, PMID 31123036]. We asked three
questions that need a continuous position along the tubule: do transporters occupy the same stretch of the PT in
both species, do paired transporters hand over at the same place, and does a gene-by-gene comparison miss part of
the picture?

**Transporters occupy different stretches.** Because COAST places every tubule cross-section on one shared axis, we
could read where along the PT a transporter is high, independent of how much of it each species' probes capture. Several
drug transporters sat at different places. SGLT2, the target of gliflozins, stayed high through about two-thirds of the
human PT but only the first third of the mouse PT. GLUT2, which carries the same glucose out of the cell, ended at the
same place in both. The anion transporters OAT1 and OAT3 were highest at the start of the human PT and persisted along
its length, whereas in mouse they peaked further along and disappeared from the last part. The cation transporter OCT2
and the urate transporter URAT1 sat later in human. Segment-level comparisons see some of these differences as changes
in amount; the axis shows them as changes in place.

**Handovers between transporters sit later in human.** Many transporters work in pairs in which one member is replaced
by another along the tubule. Of 53 such pairs, 13 switched at a different position in human, and 8 of these switched
later, because the early human partner stayed on further along the tubule. The pairs include carriers for sugars,
urate, organic cations and neutral and cationic amino acids, and seven of seven checked pairs reproduced in an
independent human atlas. A comparison of the three segment averages recovered only a fraction of each shift,
because several switches move within S2. Positions inside a segment are exactly what a continuous coordinate adds.

**A gene-by-gene comparison misses mouse-specific copies.** Species comparisons usually keep only genes with one copy
in each species. Mouse genes from families that expanded in mouse, such as the cytochrome P450 and UDP-glucuronosyl-
transferase families, were two to four times more often strongly zonated along the PT than one-to-one genes. Part of
the mouse PT's positional organisation therefore lives in gene copies that have no single human counterpart and drop out
of standard comparisons.

**Implications.** These results suggest that the mouse PT is not a scaled copy of the human PT.
- **Glucose.** A longer SGLT2 zone in human would place more of the PT under gliflozin action and less of glucose uptake
  in the later segment that SGLT1 serves [to verify]. This is a testable explanation for species differences in how
  much glucose these drugs leave in the urine.
- **Drug handling.** OAT1 and OAT3 bring many drugs into PT cells from the blood, and OCT2 brings in metformin and
  cisplatin [OCT/MATE review, PMC3675737; to verify]. Where in the PT a drug enters the cells determines which cells it injures and
  which neighbours could take up what they release, so a different layout may contribute to differences in drug
  clearance and nephrotoxicity between mouse and human.
- **Model choice.** Mouse findings about where a transporter or toxicity sits in the PT should not be transferred to
  human without checking position, and genes in mouse-expanded families need their own comparison.

**Limits.** All human tissue came from one donor and two cortical sections, so every human result needs confirmation in
more donors. Human gradients were shallower overall; we could not tell whether this is biology or measurement, so we
compared positions and never amounts. Transcript position need not match protein position, and SGLT1 itself was too
rarely detected in our human sections to measure directly. Antibody staining for SGLT2, OAT1, OCT2 and URAT1 along
human PT would test the central claims directly.

Thus, a continuous coordinate turns the question "is this transporter higher in mouse or human?" into "where in the
tubule does it work?", and for several clinically important transporters the answer differs between the species.
