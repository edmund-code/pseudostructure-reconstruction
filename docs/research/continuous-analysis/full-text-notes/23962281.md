## What the paper actually does (from the full text)
- Model: Gaussian-process regression with a hierarchy. Gene function g_n(t) ~ GP(0, k_g); each replicate f_nr(t) ~ GP(g_n(t), k_f); data = f_nr + Gaussian noise. Both kernels are squared exponential with their own amplitude and length-scale (Methods, "A hierarchy across replicates"). Deeper hierarchies add layers, e.g. gene -> experiment -> replicate (eq. 11), or cluster h_i(t) -> gene g_n(t) -> replicate f_nr(t) (eq. 12). The authors note the hierarchy "could be extended ... to model inter-species variation".
- Inference: closed-form GP posterior for the functions; hyper-parameters by type-II maximum likelihood (gradient optimisation), i.e. point estimates, not integrated over. Gaussian noise only; endnote a says other noise models break conjugacy.
- Efficiency: if all genes in a cluster are measured at the same time points, the cluster marginal likelihood depends on the cluster mean profile, reducing cost from O(N^3 D^3) to O(D^3) (eq. 15, appendix).
- Clustering: agglomerative, Bayesian-hierarchical-clustering-style greedy merging that maximises the summed log marginal likelihood, alternating with hyper-parameter optimisation (EM-like; Algorithm 1). Clusters are inferred, not given.
- Null / calibration / significance: none. No hypothesis test of a trend, of a cluster, or of a difference between conditions. Genes for Fig. 2 were ranked by the ratio of signal variance (k_g) to replicate-plus-noise variance (k_f), a diagnostic, not a test.
- Replicates: central purpose. Replicates may be sampled at different, irregular times. Variance shares per gene are reported as a diagnostic (e.g. CG18135: 87% g_n, 6% replicate, 6% noise; AP-47: 60/34/6; OstStt3: 55/36/8; Results/Fig. 2).
- Validation: Drosophila melanogaster development (8 replicates, up to 13 time points, 3,695 genes, 56 arrays). (i) Imputation of whole missing arrays: HGP RMSE lower than plain GP, replicate mean or median in every randomisation (Table 1). (ii) 100 randomly missing values: HGP beats KNN and Bayesian PCA (Table 2). (iii) Data fusion across three experiments (Fig. 5). (iv) Clustering of 300 pre-selected dynamic genes: agglomerative HGP gives higher biological homogeneity index (GO MF/BP/CC) than an agglomerative plain GP (Cooke et al.) and Mclust (Table 3). No simulation study.
- Plots: per-gene panels of g_n(t) and each replicate with 95% bands (Fig. 2); cluster-level h(t) with member genes and replicates, plus a predictive curve for a new member (Fig. 3); fused curve (Fig. 5).
- Software: Python, "available from the authors' website" (staffwww.dcs.shef.ac.uk/people/J.Hensman/). No package name is given.
- Limitations stated: clustering scales poorly with the number of genes (all pairwise merges); suggests randomised variants. Proposes future use "in models of pathway activity" with GO groupings (Conclusions). Limitations for us: Gaussian likelihood on transformed data, not NB counts with an exposure offset; with 2 specimens per group, the replicate-level kernel is barely identified for one gene (sharing it across a pathway helps); hyper-parameters are point estimates, so bands understate uncertainty; the cluster curve h(t) is driven by the cluster mean of member profiles (eq. 15), so members rising and falling still cancel unless curves are sign-aligned first; no test for a non-flat pathway curve or a group difference.

## Fit to our design (revised after reading the full text)
Conceptually the right shape for a pathway program (pathway -> gene -> specimen), and the efficient-likelihood condition (all genes measured on the same units) holds for us. But it is a Gaussian, point-estimate, test-free model fitted to inferred clusters. For us it would be a descriptive pathway curve with a band on log-scale binned profiles, built after sign-aligning or splitting members; an mgcv NB model with nested factor smooths is the more direct route with counts.
